# The buildings

Every building on the mountain is built the same way: LOW-POLY GEOMETRY CARRYING PAINTED MATERIALS. A wall is a dozen triangles, and its face carries what it is made of — larch boards, ribbed steel, a standing-seam roof, formwork concrete, glass — with that material's colour, roughness and relief, so the sun picks out a board's edge, a rib or a mullion on a flat quad.

**Why painted in code and not baked in Blender.** A baked tile and a painted one look alike at the distances a station is seen from a chair or the piste; painting keeps every building a pure function of the map like everything else on the mountain, ships no binary textures, and lets the suite hold the stack (`tests/facade_test.ts`). Blender stays the route for an object seen close and shaped beyond a box (the skis, the machines — `docs/models.md`).

## What the real ones are like

Restated from public descriptions of built stations and the operators' own literature; no building is copied.

- **A detachable chairlift's terminal** is an open shed more than a house: a long hood of white or light-grey panels over the bullwheel and the rail the chairs slow down on, on a few steel columns, the wheel turning under its end. Its panels are large composite or aluminium sheets with recessed joints; a band of colour runs round it. An operator's glazed cabin stands at the load and the unload; a mast with a lightning rod and an anemometer rises off it or the house.
- **The drive and the garage.** At one end, usually the foot, a building holds the drive and the room the chairs are run into overnight: a concrete base, timber or sheet walls, a wide roller door, small windows, a louvred vent for the motor room, a pitched roof with deep eaves for the snow.
- **A gondola station** is a hall: a concrete base (cast in place, its formwork joints and tie holes showing), a steel frame skinned in profiled sheet or timber with long bands of glass, a shallow pitched roof on wide eaves sized for the snow load; the cabins turn round the wheel under a roof on columns.
- **A drag lift's stations** are small: a timber hut for the operator and the drive, the bullwheel on a steel frame beside it.
- **Materials as they weather**: larch or spruce boards 12–20 cm wide laid vertically weather from honey-brown to silver; trapezoidal steel sheet has ribs 20–30 cm apart; a standing-seam roof's seams are about half a metre apart; concrete formwork panels are 1.2–2.4 m with tie holes in a grid.

- **The start area** is a level platform: the start gate (the wand between two posts, its switch and cable in a small housing at the post, wired to a main and a backup timer), a house or tent over it, and a ramp out of the gate falling at up to about 45°, built up some 2 m on flattish ground so the racer gathers speed at once. The platform is wide enough for the referee, the starter and the hand timers; the racer plants his poles just outside the posts.
- **A temporary finish stand** is modular scaffold: 48 mm steel tube in bays of 2–2.6 m carrying decks in rising rows, braced on the diagonal at the back and the ends, a guard rail round the top. A standing terrace rises about 0.4 m a row on treads of about 0.8 m; the risers carry the event's colours. A big finish puts up several, thousands of places in all, with scaffold towers for the cameras, the commentators and the video walls, built in about a week.
- **The video wall** hangs in a scaffold tower engineered for its weight and the wind: a frame round the screen, white side cladding, a roof, facing up the course toward the stands. **The leader's platform** is a raised deck behind the finish circle's exit on scaffold legs with a coloured valance, the leader's chair in front of a board.

- **A covered gallery** over a ski area's conveyor is built in modules: arched ribs (aluminium, steel or glued timber) carrying curved polycarbonate sheet a few millimetres thick, UV-stabilised, translucent; the small ones are about 2.2 m wide and 2.3 m high inside, with doors at the ends and side exits every couple of metres. They are rated for a metre of snow on the roof and for gale-force wind, and galvanised cheeks run along both sides a little over the snow so the groomer's blade does not reach the sheet. A curved roof sheds its snow off the steep flanks and keeps it along the flatter crown. A **road snow shed or avalanche gallery** is the heavy version — corrugated steel arches or a reinforced-concrete box — and its mouth is a deeper portal frame, often in a contrasting colour, on wing walls. The game's wind tunnels take the light gallery's skin at a road gallery's scale (a 9 m lane under an arch of about 5.3 m radius), and a big axial fan in a short drum with a flared intake ahead of the entrance.

- **A ski area's base** is a front facing the mountain: the lifts' bottom stations at the foot of the runs, a near-flat run-out ending in a plaza, and round it the lodge with its terrace looking up the runs, the ticket office on the walk from the car park, the rental and the ski school by the beginners' slope, first aid near the main lift and the road, the hotels a row back, the garage and the pump house off on the service side. The lodge's slope face is glass over a stone or concrete plinth and timber, under a broad low gable that keeps its snow; the hotels carry a balcony on every floor under eaves deep enough to shelter them; the mountain restaurant is often a single low storey of stone or dark boards with a deep slab of snow to the eave, levelled snow before it with tables, a row of red deck chairs, feather flags and skis stood in the snow; a patrol hut is small, red and flagged with a white cross, toboggans stood against it and a radio mast over it.

## The village and its streets

The ski area's village stands below the hub on the valley floor, along streets of its own. What a mountain village's streets look like in winter, restated from public road-maintenance guidance, street-design manuals and descriptions of resort villages; no village is copied:

- **The road is white.** Where the winter is cold, the streets are not cleared to the tarmac: the plough leaves a layer of packed snow on them all winter, and the traffic presses it hard and smooth. It is GRITTED rather than salted — crushed stone chippings of a few millimetres, brown-grey, which the tyres throw out of the wheel tracks, so the grit lies thickest beside and between them; it is swept up in the spring.
- **The wheel tracks.** Each lane is pressed into two RUTS a car's track apart (some 1.5–1.6 m), darker, polished and a little wetted where the tyres run; between them and at the crown a ridge of looser, whiter snow. Ruts deeper than about 15 cm are cut down by the grader.
- **The plough's windrows.** What the plough throws off the carriageway lies along its edges as a bank — about 0.3 m along a shopping street where loaders lift it away, up to a metre and more along a road out of the village — LUMPY, never a clean prism, broken where a junction, a crossing or a driveway is opened (a driveway's mouth is left with a small heap of some 30 cm that its owner clears), and banked up to about 0.75 m at the corners of the junctions.
- **The widths.** One lane is 2.75–3.5 m (3.5 m is a vehicle's room on a single-lane passage); a village street wall to wall is about 10 m, a residential one less; a car-parking strip along the kerb is about 2–2.5 m, a bay 2.5 × 5 m.
- **The sidewalks** are raised behind a KERB of granite, about 100–120 mm over the road (160–200 mm at a bus stop, so the bus's floor meets it), dropped to some 15 mm at a crossing so a pram or a wheelchair rolls over. They are 1.5–2.5 m wide (at least 1.5 m is kept clear in winter), paved in granite SETTS or flags, and are cleared too but never bare: a skin of packed, gritted snow lies on them with the stone showing through where the feet run, and the snow from them is thrown onto the windrow at the kerb. Railings stand only where the walk drops away beside it.
- **Crossings** are zebra-striped where there is paint to see, at the junctions and in front of the square, the kerb dropped at both ends and the windrows opened there; a sign stands beside them.
- **The lamps** are lanterns on columns of 4.5–6 m on the sidewalk side, some 30–37 m apart and one at every junction, LED of a few thousand lumens each, warm white, lighting a residential street to some 5–7.5 lux; the road out of the village is unlit.
- **Snow poles** along a road out stand about a metre off its edge, 1.2–2 m tall, orange or banded with reflective tape, so the plough's driver finds the edge under new snow.
- **How the buildings meet the street.** Shops stand at the back of the sidewalk, their glazed fronts and awnings on it; houses stand back behind a yard of snow, a path or a drive cut through the windrow to their door. A resort's base keeps the cars from the walking: a day car park at the village's entrance, the ski bus's stop on the main street with a shelter, and a square at the main lift where the street meets the walk to the lifts.
- **The pace on them** in winter: a car at a walking village's 30 km/h or less, a bicycle at some 12 km/h, a dog walked at 4–6 km/h.

The game's streets are laid to those numbers (`defs/village-streets.ts`): a MAIN street of two 3.5 m lanes with a 2.2 m sidewalk either side behind a 0.12 m kerb and a low windrow; a BACK street of two 2.9 m lanes, a parking strip and one sidewalk, its windrows taller; CROSS streets between them; a ROAD out of two 3.25 m lanes with metre-tall banks and snow poles; the car park's aisles with their bays; a square at the main lift; a lamp every thirty-odd metres and at every junction; crossings at the junctions and the square; the bus stop with its shelter on the main street. The buildings stand on lots along them — the base's own (the lodge, the ticket office, the rental, the ski school, first aid, the hotels) on the main street round the square, and the town's (shops on the main street at the back of the sidewalk, houses, apartments and a church behind) — 15 to 40 of them.

## The log buildings

The cabins, the chalets, the woodsheds and the afterski lodge (`cabin-shapes.ts`, `lodge-shapes.ts`, out of `cabin-parts.ts`'s pieces) keep a builder of their own — a round log is a faceted loft, not a kit box — but they are drawn in the same stack by the same material, every vertex naming its layer (`facadeLayer`, `facadeUv`, marks on the trees' bench). What a log building is made of decides what stays geometry and what is paint:

- **The logs stay geometry.** A round-log wall is read by its courses' shadow lines and by the saddle-notched corners, every log run on past the corner far enough that the notch holds and its sawn end shows. A whole course is one six-sided log of sixteen triangles (its underside never drawn, its top's flat left out wherever the next course sits on it), so the silhouette costs little; the bark is painted along it (`bark`: the drawknifed grain, knots, a check) and the rings on its end (`endGrain`: the pith, the growth rings, a radial check, the bark ring).
- **The detail between is paint.** Chinking is about an inch of daub between the courses; the window, the door, the stone, the firewood and a balcony's cut boards are flat faces in their own material: `casement` (the sash, a cross of mullions, the glass — the ONLY part the night lights, the material masking the glow to the glass), `plankDoor` (four boards, two ledges and a brace, the latch), `boardShutter` (tinted green or red), `stone` (dressed courses, running bond, raked joints — the plinth and the chimneys), `woodpile` (rounds, halves and wedges end on), `balustrade` (butted boards, a waisted shape sawn out of every other joint), `render` and `quoins` (a chalet's rendered floor and its corner stones), `timber` (posts, rails, casings, the soffit). The casings, reveals and sills stay a few faces proud of the wall so a window keeps its depth.
- **The far cut is the near one painted flat.** Past `NEAR` (`cabins-view.ts`) a building's walls are one face each in `logWall` (the courses where the near cut's lie, chinked between), its windows, shutters and doors painted on them — so the hand-over is close to invisible and comes nearer the lens than when the far cut was banded colour.

## The lab

`make buildings` (or `make world ARGS="--views=stations --free"`) draws `previews/world-free-stations.png`: a chair's, a gondola's and a drag's foot and top (the longest lift of each kind on the map) from three sides, through the game's own renderer. `station-<kind>-<end>` is one of them at 1280 × 720. `--hour=21` shows the panes lit after dark. `race-buildings` (with `race-house`, `race-hut`, `race-stand`, `race-leader`, `race-screen`, `race-house-in`) is the race sheet: the start hut and the finish arena on a default run, the start house with `--slalom --hour=12`. `tunnels` (with `tunnel-mouth`, `tunnel-span`, `tunnel-exit`, `pwa/src/tools/tunnel-view.ts`) is the wind tunnels' sheet on a free ride (`--free`): the first tunnel's fan house, its gallery halfway down and its exit from three sides, and a row from inside the lane. `village` (with `village-plaza`, `village-lift`, `village-<kind>`, `pwa/src/tools/village-view.ts`) is the ski area's own buildings' sheet on a free ride: every kind from its front and back three quarters and from far off, the base from over the plaza and from up the mountain. `village-air` (the street plan from the air), `village-street` (down the main street at eye height), `village-square` (the square from the far sidewalk) and `village-back` (the back street) are the streets' views; `--hour=21` lights the lamps. The sheets replace the canvas once drawn, so name single views before them.

The log buildings have a lab of their own: `make cabins` draws every kind from six sides, at its far cut and at night (`previews/cabins.png`), with each cut's triangles; `make world ARGS="--free --views=cabin,cabin-near,cabins-air"` shows them where they stand.
